Engage, then measure.

The estimate adds up what the browser reports. It updates while you play and costs nothing, but it misses what the browser does not know: the interface's converters and driver buffers and, where Input says “not reported”, the whole input side. The real figure is usually higher.

The estimate adds up what the audio driver reports: the input and output buffers, and the wait between them (counted as input). It updates while you play and costs nothing, but it misses what the driver does not report, such as the interface's converters. The real figure is usually a little higher.

The measurement is the real string-to-ear figure. Connect a cable from your interface's output to the input selected in Klirr, turn your speakers or headphones down, mute your strings and press Measure. Klirr replaces the amp with a few short clicks for a second or two and times how long each takes to come back. It runs only when you press the button and adds nothing to the signal path.

Dropouts are counted when the audio thread falls behind and never catches up. You may hear a tick or a gap, and the sound that arrived in the meantime now waits in a buffer, so the latency grows by that much and stays there. If the latency grows and dropouts are counted, close other programs and browser tabs, or try another browser. If it grows without dropouts, the browser is adding buffering on its own.

Dropouts are counted when the output finds too little input waiting. You hear a short gap, and Klirr keeps a little more input in reserve from then on, so the latency grows by that much and stays there until you engage again. If dropouts keep coming, close other programs that use audio, or choose a larger buffer under Settings.

What to expect

  • WASAPI exclusive (Windows) runs your interface at its smallest buffer, without the Windows audio engine in between. It takes the interface for Klirr alone: other programs cannot play on it while Klirr runs.
  • WASAPI shared goes through the Windows audio engine and lets other programs play along, but it is slow: about 90 ms measured on a Focusrite Scarlett. Use it to try Klirr out, then switch to exclusive or ASIO.
  • ASIO, where available, talks to your interface's own driver. Choose the buffer size under Settings, or keep the driver's own (set in its control panel).
  • Core Audio (macOS) lets Klirr choose its own buffer under Settings. JACK or PipeWire (Linux) run every app at the server's buffer size; set it there.
  • Bluetooth headphones or speakers add 100–300 ms. Use wired headphones, ideally plugged into your interface.
  • macOS usually gives the lowest latency. Core Audio lets every app run with small buffers, so Safari and Chrome both benefit.
  • Windows browsers cannot use ASIO; they go through the Windows audio engine. Chrome and Edge are typically around 20–40 ms. Firefox buffers more on the input side and is often around 50 ms: fine for trying out the amp.
  • Linux depends on the sound server (PipeWire or PulseAudio) and its buffer size.
  • Bluetooth headphones or speakers add 100–300 ms. Use wired headphones, ideally plugged into your interface.
  • Run your interface at the same sample rate as the system (48 kHz), and close other programs that use audio.

The planned desktop app talks to ASIO and Core Audio directly and aims for about 5–10 ms.
